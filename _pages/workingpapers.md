**The Politics of Counterterrorism: Historical Legacies and Institutional Change** 

Why do states change how they prosecute terrorism? After 9/11, governments around the world expanded counterterrorism efforts, but their responses often relied on institutions established long before the War on Terror. This paper argues that changes in terrorism prosecution reflect both historical institutional legacies and shifts in domestic political power. Earlier colonial and international security relationships shaped the courts and legal procedures available to governments, while subsequent political developments influenced how these institutions were adapted. As regimes become more authoritarian, governments are more likely to expand executive control over terrorism prosecutions through specialized courts and exceptional legal procedures. Drawing on comparative historical research in Egypt, Morocco, Jordan, and Saudi Arabia, alongside a developing cross-national dataset, the paper examines how inherited security institutions adapt to new international pressures and changing domestic political conditions.

**The Rise of the Madani Party: The Evolution of Political Parties in the Middle East** (with Marsin Alshamary)

What explains the rise of madani parties in the Middle East and North Africa? After the Arab Spring, there was a proliferation of political parties across the region. Many of these parties self-identified as “madani” parties, a designation organic to the Middle East that typically connotes a mild political secularism and a focus on anti-corruption, institutions, and the rule of law. In this paper, we argue that madani parties emerge in response to the perceived governing failures of Islamist parties. As a result, madani parties define themselves through the rejection of ideological conflict. They prioritize a procedural and technocratic vision of governance and eschew positive ideological projects. In practice, these parties have proliferated in number in recent years but have generally been weak and fragmented due to their lack of clear political programs. Empirically, we examine the establishment and trajectory of parties in the Middle East through the construction of a novel dataset and, subsequently, through comparative analysis of parties in Egypt and Iraq. We conclude with a discussion of the consequences of madani party proliferation for opposition politics in the region.

**Rethinking the Moderate-Radical Dichotomy in Opposition Politics**  

This article reassesses how scholars conceptualize moderation and radicalization in the study of opposition politics. Examining 62 works on Islamist inclusion and moderation published between 1994 and 2026, I show that scholars use different substantive criteria – including support for democracy, pluralism, nonviolence, and de-Islamization – to define the same dependent variable. These heterogeneous definitions embed normative preferences into the concept of moderation, obscuring the political contexts in which opposition actors operate and helping produce contradictory findings about whether inclusion or exclusion causes moderation or radicalization. I propose instead a relational conceptualization that defines moderation and radicalization according to the extent and pace of systemic transformation that actors seek. This approach provides a common benchmark for distinguishing behavioral from ideological change and makes political constraints central to interpreting observed behavior, permitting scholars to highlight how strategies and preferences of opposition actors evolve over time. More broadly, it allows scholars to study democracy, pluralism, violence, and ideology as concepts that are potentially related to, but analytically distinct from, moderation.

**Why States Keep Fighting: Regeneration, Endurance, and the Wars on Iran**

Why do weaker states continue fighting against much stronger adversaries, and when does military pressure compel them to concede? Existing theories of coercion emphasize how states update their expectations about the costs and benefits of continued fighting. This paper argues that relative rates of military regeneration—the ability to replace the capabilities needed to sustain a military strategy—are an important source of information shaping these calculations. Unlike weapons stockpiles or battlefield outcomes, regeneration rates indicate how the balance of military power may change over time. When weaker states can replenish essential capabilities while forcing stronger adversaries to expend difficult-to-replace assets, military superiority may fail to produce coercive leverage. Drawing on the 2025 and 2026 wars involving Iran, the paper examines how missile and drone production, battlefield attrition, and the depletion of defensive interceptors shaped the prospects for continued fighting. It argues that relative regeneration rates help explain wartime endurance and why even large military power advantages may fail to compel weaker states to concede.

**Mobilization without Organization** (with Alexei Abrahams and Dana El Kurd)  

Since their rise in the 2000s, social media have had a complicated relationship with social movements. Although they offer unprecedented connectivity, they may also facilitate “mobilization without organization” (Tufekci 2017) or “mobilization without movement” (Yom 2022), generating fragile movements that lack organizational maturity. This paper contributes original evidence from the Palestinian struggle, which is reliant on social media because of geographical diffuseness, the co-optation of formal institutions, and the repression of political parties. We investigate by scraping Twitter and Instagram, conducting face-to-face interviews with activists, and documenting protest data. Our analysis suggests that movements heavily reliant on social media are not necessarily doomed to this trap, as civil society organizations have acquired a growing online footprint. Yet this new de facto structure is emerging organically, without a formal process for conferring authority or ensuring accountability.
